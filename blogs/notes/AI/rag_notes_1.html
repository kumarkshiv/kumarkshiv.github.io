Part 1 · The simplest explanation

What RAG is, in plain words

RAG means: before the AI answers, look up the relevant facts in your own documents and hand them to the AI together with the question.

The problem

You ask ChatGPT: “What's the reimbursement policy for home office setup?” ChatGPT has never seen your company's private policy document. So it does what LLMs do when they lack information: it hallucinates. It gives a confident, generic answer that sounds right for “most companies” but may be wrong for yours.

The real issue is missing context. The model is smart, but it does not have the facts.

The manual fix you have probably already done

  1. Open your company's policy document and find the section about home office setup.
  2. Copy that section and paste it into ChatGPT along with your question: “Refer to this policy.”
  3. ChatGPT now answers correctly, because the facts are right there in the prompt.

Those three steps are RAG. Each one has a name:

Retrieval

Looking up your documents and pulling out the relevant section.

Augmentation

Improving (“augmenting”) the prompt by adding the retrieved text to it.

Generation

The LLM generates a response based on the augmented prompt.

What a “RAG system” actually means

You don't want every employee to dig through documents themselves. A RAG system automates the three steps. The user only types the question. The system finds the right policy text, builds the improved prompt, and asks the LLM. The rest of the course is about how each step is automated.

Part 2 · When to use RAG, and when not to

Prompt engineering, fine-tuning or RAG?

RAG is not the answer to everything. Prompting sets the rules, fine-tuning sets the style, RAG supplies the facts.

A common beginner mistake is treating RAG as the fix for every AI problem. There are three main ways to get better answers from an AI, and each solves a different problem. The video uses the Policy Copilot to show all three.

What the Policy Copilot needs

  1. Restrictions and security. Don't answer some topics (performance-review appeals, salary discussions). Send those people to HR. Never reveal personal employee information.
    Solution: prompt engineering
    These are strict written instructions given to the LLM, a “rule book” that keeps the bot safe and professional.
  2. A specific voice. Sound like the CEO, who is Scottish and speaks with warm, blunt candour. Just saying “reply in a Scottish accent” isn't enough; it must use his words and tone.
    Solution: fine-tuning
    Collect his speeches, emails, blog posts and videos, and further train a model on hundreds of example questions and answers in his style.
  3. Accurate, current policy answers.
    Solution: RAG
    Retrieve the latest policy text at the moment the question is asked.

Why not just fine-tune the model on all the policies?

  • Policies change. Every change means retraining the model again.
  • Training is slow and expensive. It takes time and lots of computing power.
  • No citations. Users can't see which document an answer came from, so they can't verify it.
  • Accuracy drops. The video notes that the more facts you push into training data, the less reliably the model recalls any one of them.
  • Knowledge cutoff. A trained model only knows what was in its training data, frozen at that date.

RAG avoids all five because it fetches information at query time, not at training time. Update a document and the very next answer uses it.

MethodWhat it changesBest forBad forCost to update
Prompt engineeringThe instructions sent with each requestRules, refusals, output format, tone hintsLarge amounts of private factsEdit text. Instant.
Fine-tuningThe model's weights (it is retrained)Stable patterns: writing style, voice, format habitsFacts that change; citationsRetrain. Slow and costly.
RAGThe context the model sees per questionPrivate, changing, verifiable factsTeaching a style or behaviourRe-index the changed document. Minutes.

Part 3 · Retrieval, method 1

Keyword search: TF-IDF and BM25

Keyword search finds documents that contain the exact words of the question, and ranks them by how often and how uniquely those words appear.

Step by step

  1. Pick keywords from the question. From “What's the reimbursement policy for home office setup?” we keep reimbursement and home office. Filler words like “what”, “the”, “for” (called stop words) are ignored.
  2. Search the documents for those words. The simplest way is the Linux grep command, or an SQL LIKE query if the text is in a database.
  3. Rank the matches. Count how often each keyword appears in each document. The document with the most (and most meaningful) matches wins.
bash · grep
# -r: search all files in the folder, -i: ignore case, -l: list file names
grep -ril "reimbursement" techcorp-docs/
grep -ril "home office" techcorp-docs/
sql
SELECT * FROM policies
WHERE content LIKE '%reimbursement%'
   OR content LIKE '%home office%';

Both only return text that matches the words exactly. That is why the chance of getting the right answer every time is low. Real search engines use smarter scoring. The two most popular are TF-IDF and BM25.

TF-IDF (Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency)

  • TF, term frequency: how many times the word appears in this document. More mentions suggests the document is about that word.
  • IDF, inverse document frequency: how rare the word is across all documents. A word in every document (like “office” or “policy”) tells you nothing, so it gets a low weight. A word in only one document (like “furniture”) is a strong signal, so it gets a high weight.
  • Score = TF × IDF for every word, then the query and each document are compared (with cosine similarity, explained in Part 5).

Formula used by scikit-learn: idf(w) = ln((1 + N) / (1 + df(w))) + 1, where N is the number of documents and df(w) is how many documents contain w.

BM25 (Best Match 25)

BM25 is an improved TF-IDF and is the default in search engines like Elasticsearch. It adds two fixes:

  • Saturation (parameter k1 ≈ 1.2–2). The 10th mention of a word adds much less than the 1st. A document can't win by spamming a word.
  • Length normalisation (parameter b ≈ 0.75). A long document naturally contains more words, so its counts are scaled down. Short focused documents aren't punished.

In the video, BM25 gives “office” a score of zero because it appears in all three test sentences, so BM25 treats it as useless. TF-IDF still gives it 0.4. That is the “BM25 is more strict” point.

python · TF-IDF with scikit-learn
# pip install scikit-learn
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity

docs = [
    "Office equipment policy for remote staff",
    "Office furniture guidelines",
    "Office travel policy",
]

analyzer = TfidfVectorizer()                # the "TF-IDF analyzer"
doc_vectors = analyzer.fit_transform(docs)  # learn vocabulary + score every word
print(analyzer.get_feature_names_out())     # the words it found
print(doc_vectors.toarray())                # 2-D array: one row per doc, one column per word

query_vector = analyzer.transform(["furniture"])
scores = cosine_similarity(query_vector, doc_vectors)[0]
print(scores)   # highest score -> document 2 (furniture guidelines)
python · BM25 with rank_bm25
# pip install rank_bm25
from rank_bm25 import BM25Okapi

tokenized_docs = [d.lower().split() for d in docs]   # BM25 needs lists of words
bm25 = BM25Okapi(tokenized_docs)                       # build the BM25 index

scores = bm25.get_scores("furniture".split())
print(scores)   # document 2 wins again; "office" alone would score ~0

Part 4 · Retrieval, method 2

Semantic search and embeddings

Semantic search finds text by meaning, so “allowance” can find a document that only says “reimbursement”.

Why keyword search is not enough

If the exact word isn't in the document, keyword search fails. Users don't know which words the policy writer used:

  • User says allowance, document says reimbursement.
  • User says work from home, document says home office.
  • User says desk, document says furniture.

This is called the vocabulary mismatch problem. Semantic search fixes it.

How semantic search works, step by step

  1. Turn text into numbers. An embedding model converts each piece of text into a list of numbers called a vector (or an embedding).
  2. Numbers capture meaning. Think of each number as a score for one aspect of meaning. The video's example: maybe the first number says how animal-related the text is, the second how workplace-related it is, and so on.
  3. Similar meaning ends up close together. Treat each vector as coordinates of a point in space. “Dogs” and “pets” land near each other in the animal area. “Remote” lands somewhere else.
  4. Search = find the nearest points. Embed the question the same way, then return the documents whose points are closest to it.
Honest detail

In real models the numbers are learned automatically and are not human-readable. Nobody decides “number 1 means animals”. The idea that each number is one aspect of meaning is a helpful mental picture, not literally true.

Part 5 · The maths, made simple

Dot product and cosine similarity

To compare two vectors we multiply them number by number, add up the results, and scale by their lengths. The closer to 1, the more similar the meaning.

In 2 dimensions you can see which points are close. The model we use has 384 dimensions, which nobody can picture. So we need a formula that works in any number of dimensions. That formula is the dot product, a “mathematical ruler” for high-dimensional space.

Step by step, with the video's numbers

Pretend each sentence has a 2-number vector:

  • A: “Dogs are allowed in the office” = (1, 5)
  • B: the pets sentence = (2, 4)
  • C: the remote-work sentence = (6, 1)
  1. Multiply matching positions. A·B: 1×2 = 2 and 5×4 = 20.
  2. Add them. 2 + 20 = 22. That is the dot product. Likewise A·C = 1×6 + 5×1 = 11, and B·C = 2×6 + 4×1 = 16.
  3. Normalise. Divide by the lengths of both vectors (length = √(x² + y²)). This removes the effect of size so only direction matters. The result is the cosine similarity.
  4. Read the score. A·B = 22 ÷ (5.10 × 4.47) = 0.965 (very similar). A·C = 0.355 and B·C = 0.588. So A and B are the closest pair, exactly as the video concludes.
Precise version

Cosine similarity ranges from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (same direction). The video says “between 0 and 1” because typical text embeddings rarely go negative. A raw dot product equals cosine similarity only when both vectors have length 1. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 outputs length-1 vectors, which is why the video can use np.dot directly.

python · sentence similarity
# pip install sentence-transformers numpy
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np

# downloads the model (~90 MB) once, loads 22M parameters into memory
model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

sentences = [
    "Dogs are allowed in the office on Fridays",
    "Pets are welcome at work",
    "Employees can work remotely",
]
emb = model.encode(sentences)   # shape (3, 384); vectors already have length 1

print("dogs vs pets  :", np.dot(emb[0], emb[1]))
print("dogs vs remote:", np.dot(emb[0], emb[2]))
print("pets vs remote:", np.dot(emb[1], emb[2]))
Part 6 · The tool that makes vectors

Embedding models

An embedding model reads text and outputs a fixed-length list of numbers that represents its meaning. It does not write text.

Finding one

  1. Machine-learning models are grouped by use case: computer vision, natural language processing (NLP), audio, and more.
  2. Hugging Face is the popular website where you find models, datasets and demo apps.
  3. Inside NLP, the task we need is Sentence Similarity. A popular model there is sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2.
Output size384

numbers per sentence (a 384-dimensional dense vector)

Parameters22 M

learned values that store its “knowledge” of language

Download size~90 MB

small enough to run on a normal laptop, even without a GPU

What is a parameter?

A parameter is one number the model learned during training. Together, the parameters encode how words relate, what sentences mean and which concepts are similar. More parameters means more “brain power”, but also a bigger, slower, costlier model.

Why such a small model is enough: an embedding model only has to map meaning to numbers. GPT models have to reason and write fluent text, which needs far more capacity. In a RAG system you typically use a small embedding model for retrieval and a large LLM for generation.

Golden rule

Use the same embedding model for your documents and your questions. Vectors from two different models live in different spaces and cannot be compared.

To choose a model, check the MTEB embedding leaderboard on Hugging Face. When the video was recorded, Gemini and Qwen3 embedding models were near the top. Rankings change often, so look at the current list.

Part 7 · Storing and searching millions of vectors

Vector databases

A vector database stores embeddings and finds the nearest ones to a question fast, using an index instead of checking every vector.

The scale problem

Comparing the question with three sentences is easy. The Policy Copilot has hundreds of long documents. With 500 documents at 384 dimensions, comparing the question with every stored vector costs 500 × 384 = 192,000 multiplications per question. That is like finding a number in a phone book by reading every page. Fine for a small book, hopeless for millions of entries.

What a vector database does

Think of it as a smart librarian who knows exactly which shelf to check. It is fast, uses resources efficiently and scales, because of indexing.

Indexing means organising the vectors into neighbourhoods ahead of time. Animal policies sit together, health benefits together, remote-work policies together. When someone asks about bringing a dog to work, the search goes straight to the animal neighbourhood and only compares there.

The three indexing algorithms named in the video

AlgorithmIdea in one lineEveryday analogy
HNSW
Hierarchical Navigable Small World		A graph where each vector links to its most similar neighbours, in layers from sparse to dense. Search starts at an entry point and hops toward closer and closer neighbours. Fast and accurate, so it is the default in most vector databases.Express highway to the right city, then local roads to the right street.
IVF
Inverted File index		Split vectors into clusters, each with a centre. Compare the question with the centres, then search only the closest cluster(s).Go to the right aisle in a library, then scan that aisle.
LSH
Locality-Sensitive Hashing		Hash functions that put similar vectors into the same “bucket”. Search only the question's bucket.Sorting letters into pigeonholes by postcode.

HNSW at a glance

The search takes big jumps on the sparse top layer, drops down a layer, takes smaller jumps, and finishes on the bottom layer where every vector lives. It visits a handful of nodes instead of all of them.

Interview extra

These indexes do approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) search. They trade a tiny bit of accuracy (they might occasionally miss the true closest vector) for a huge gain in speed. Checking every vector is called exact or flat search.

Popular vector databases

Chroma

Open source, Python-friendly, free, runs on your laptop. Best for learning, students and small projects.

Pinecone

Fully managed cloud service: they run the infrastructure, you send data and queries. Used in production by large companies. Pay per use.

Weaviate

Open-source vector database with a GraphQL API. Another solid production option.

Using ChromaDB, step by step

  1. Install: pip install chromadb
  2. Create a client (the connection to the database).
  3. Create a collection called policies. A collection is like a table. Chroma prepares storage for vectors and metadata, and sets up its default embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2).
  4. Add documents with collection.add(). For each one, Chroma converts the text to a 384-number vector, saves it, and inserts it into the HNSW index. It is searchable immediately.
  5. Query with collection.query(). Chroma embeds the question with the same model and returns the nearest documents.
python · ChromaDB
import chromadb

client = chromadb.Client()                       # in-memory: data is lost when the program stops
collection = client.create_collection(name="policies")

collection.add(
    ids=["pets-1", "home-office-1"],             # every entry needs a unique id
    documents=[
        "Dogs are allowed in the office on Fridays.",
        "Remote staff can claim up to $500 for home office furniture.",
    ],
    metadatas=[{"source": "pet_policy.md"}, {"source": "home_office_setup.md"}],
)

results = collection.query(query_texts=["Can I bring my dog?"], n_results=1)
print(results["documents"], results["distances"])

Make it persistent

chromadb.Client() keeps everything in RAM, so your data disappears when the program ends. Fine for learning, not for production. Use PersistentClient with a folder path so data survives restarts.

Change the embedding model

Pass an embedding_function when creating the collection, e.g. OpenAI's embeddings with your API key, or any custom function.

python · persistent client + custom embeddings
import chromadb
from chromadb.utils import embedding_functions

client = chromadb.PersistentClient(path="./chroma_db")   # saved to disk

openai_ef = embedding_functions.OpenAIEmbeddingFunction(
    api_key="YOUR_OPENAI_API_KEY",
    model_name="text-embedding-3-small",
)
collection = client.get_or_create_collection(
    name="policies",
    embedding_function=openai_ef,
)

Part 8 · Cutting big documents into searchable pieces

Chunking

Chunking splits long documents into small focused pieces, so a search returns the one paragraph that answers the question instead of the whole handbook.

The precision problem

Imagine a 50-page employee handbook stored as one entry. Chroma will happily create one embedding for it. But when someone asks “What's the remote work policy?”, the result is the entire 50 pages. The user (and the LLM) must dig through mostly irrelevant text. With chunking, the same search returns only the remote-work section.

Chunking strategies

  • Fixed-size chunks: cut every N characters (e.g. 500). Simple and reliable, but can cut sentences in half: “Dogs are allowed” ends up in one chunk and “on Fridays” in the next.
  • Overlap: repeat the last ~50 characters of each chunk at the start of the next. If a sentence gets cut, both chunks still carry the context.
  • Sentence-based: each sentence is a chunk.
  • Paragraph-based: each paragraph is a chunk.
  • Recursive (LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter): tries to split at paragraphs first, then lines, then sentences, then words, and packs pieces up to the size limit. The usual default.
  • Semantic and agentic chunking: split by changes in meaning, or let an LLM decide. Mentioned in the video as out of scope.

The balance

Too small

Context is lost. Information is fragmented, so the answer is incomplete (“Dogs are allowed” without “on Fridays”).

Too large

Poor precision. Lots of irrelevant text, slower search, and overwhelming results. You are back to the original problem.

Guidelines from the video

  • Size: 200–500 characters per chunk is a good balance of context and precision.
  • Overlap: 50–100 characters to keep continuity.
  • Boundaries: split at sentence ends, never mid-word, and keep paragraphs together where possible.
  • Content type matters: technical documents may need different sizes than general policies.
  • Test with real questions: check that chunks actually answer them, that overlap preserves meaning, and adjust the size by watching search results.

How chunks go into the vector database

  1. Chunk the large document.
  2. Add each chunk to the collection with a unique id (e.g. handbook-chunk-0, handbook-chunk-1) and metadata such as the source file.
  3. At query time, the search returns the specific chunks that are most relevant.
python · simple chunker with overlap (no libraries)
def chunk_text(text, chunk_size=500, overlap=50):
    chunks, start = [], 0
    while start < len(text):
        end = min(start + chunk_size, len(text))
        # try to end at a sentence boundary inside this window
        if end != len(text):
            last_period = text.rfind(". ", start, end)
            if last_period != -1 and last_period + 1 - start >= chunk_size // 2:
                end = last_period + 1
        chunks.append(text[start:end].strip())
        if end == len(text):          # reached the end of the document
            break
        start = max(end - overlap, start + 1)   # step back to create the overlap
    return chunks

The complete RAG pipeline

RAG runs in two phases: an offline phase that prepares the documents once, and an online phase that runs for every question.

Chunking, embedding and storing thousands of documents takes a long time. So all of that happens before users start asking questions. The video calls this preparation the RAG pipeline (you will also hear “ingestion” or “indexing pipeline”).

Phase 1 · offline, done once
  1. Load the policy documents.
  2. Chunk them (size 500, overlap 50).
  3. Embed each chunk (the video uses OpenAI's embedding model).
  4. Store vectors + text + metadata in the vector DB.
Phase 2 · online, every question
  1. Embed the user's question with the same model.
  2. Retrieve the top-k most similar chunks.
  3. Augment a prompt: rules + chunks + question.
  4. Generate the answer with the LLM and return it.

# pip install chromadb openai langchain-text-splitters
import chromadb
from openai import OpenAI
from langchain_text_splitters import RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter

llm = OpenAI()   # reads OPENAI_API_KEY

# ---------- Phase 1: offline indexing ----------
def build_index(documents):                          # documents = {"file.md": "text", ...}
    splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(chunk_size=500, chunk_overlap=50)
    collection = chromadb.PersistentClient(path="./chroma_db").get_or_create_collection("policies")
    for name, text in documents.items():
        chunks = splitter.split_text(text)
        collection.add(
            ids=[f"{name}-{i}" for i in range(len(chunks))],
            documents=chunks,
            metadatas=[{"source": name}] * len(chunks),
        )
    return collection

# ---------- Phase 2: online, per question ----------
def retrieve(collection, question, k=3):
    hits = collection.query(query_texts=[question], n_results=k)
    return list(zip(hits["documents"][0], hits["metadatas"][0]))

def augment(question, chunks):
    context = "\n".join(f"[{m['source']}] {text}" for text, m in chunks)
    return f"""You are TechCorp's Policy Copilot.
Answer ONLY from the context. If the answer is not there, say you don't know.
Never discuss salaries or performance-review appeals; send those to HR.
Cite the source file in brackets.

Context:
{context}

Question: {question}
Answer:"""

def generate(prompt):
    reply = llm.chat.completions.create(
        model="YOUR_CHAT_MODEL",                  # any chat model you have access to
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
    )
    return reply.choices[0].message.content

def rag_pipeline(collection, question):
    return generate(augment(question, retrieve(collection, question)))

Part 10 · Production: speed

Caching

Caching stores the result of expensive work so the next identical (or similar) question is answered instantly.

Why RAG is slow

Every question triggers several expensive operations: generating an embedding, searching the vector database, and calling the LLM API. Without optimisation one query can take nearly a second (~950 ms). Yet people ask the same things again and again: “What's the reimbursement policy for home office setup?” gets asked dozens of times. A cached reply can take about 5 ms, which is 190× faster.

The four cache types

CacheStoresHelps when
Query cacheComplete question → answer pairsFrequently asked questions repeat word for word. The simplest one.
Embedding cacheText → its vectorThe same text gets embedded repeatedly (policy chunks reused across searches).
Vector search cacheQuery → retrieved chunksDifferent wordings return the same chunks (“remote work” and “working from home”).
LLM response cachePrompt → generated answerLLM calls are the slowest and most expensive step, so this cache is the most valuable.

Cache at the right level: not too granular, not too broad, with sensible expiry times.

Redis and TTL

Redis is a very fast in-memory key-value store, popular for caching because it supports many data types and has built-in expiry.

  1. Make a key: hash the question (and context) into a short fixed string, e.g. with SHA-256.
  2. Check the cache first. If the key exists, return the stored answer immediately (cache hit).
  3. Otherwise run the full RAG pipeline (cache miss), then store the answer under the key with an expiry time.

TTL (time to live) is that expiry time. Long enough to give speed benefits, short enough that answers don't go stale after a policy changes. For slow-changing policy documents something like an hour works; dynamic content needs shorter TTLs.

Precise version

The video says hashing lets “similar queries share the same entry”. A hash only matches identical text (after any normalisation such as lowercasing). Sharing between differently-worded but similar questions needs a semantic cache: embed the question and reuse a cached answer when cosine similarity is above a threshold. The simulator below lets you try both.

# pip install redis    (and run a Redis server, e.g. docker run -p 6379:6379 redis)
import hashlib
import redis

cache = redis.Redis(host="localhost", port=6379, decode_responses=True)
TTL_SECONDS = 3600   # 1 hour

def cached_rag(question, collection):
    normalized = question.strip().lower()
    key = "rag:" + hashlib.sha256(normalized.encode()).hexdigest()

    answer = cache.get(key)
    if answer is not None:                 # HIT: ~5 ms
        return answer

    answer = rag_pipeline(collection, question)   # MISS: ~950 ms
    cache.setex(key, TTL_SECONDS, answer)          # store with expiry
    return answer
Part 11 · Production: visibility

Monitoring

You can't manage what you don't measure. Monitoring tracks how fast and how well the RAG system works, and alerts you when it doesn't.

General metrics

  • Response time (latency): how fast questions are answered.
  • Throughput: how many queries are handled per second.
  • Error rate: what percentage of requests fail.

RAG-specific metrics

  • Retrieval quality: how relevant the returned chunks are to the question.
  • Embedding performance: how long generating vectors takes.
  • Chunking efficiency: how well documents are being split.

Alert thresholds tell you immediately when something is wrong. The video's examples: response time above 2 seconds means a performance issue; error rate above 5% means a system problem. Base thresholds on real measured performance, not theory, to avoid alert fatigue (so many false alarms that people stop paying attention).

Part 12 · Production: reliability

Error handling and graceful degradation

Things will break. A good RAG system still gives the user some useful answer instead of a crash.

In production, vector databases go down, LLM services become unavailable, and networks time out. The goal is graceful degradation: keep working at reduced capability. The video's cascading fallback strategy tries each option in turn:

  1. Full RAG pipeline.
  2. If that fails, keyword search.
  3. If that fails, return the retrieved chunks directly (no LLM summary).
  4. If that fails, simple text matching.
  5. Last resort: a helpful error message.

Circuit breaker

If a service keeps failing, calling it again and again wastes time and makes things worse. A circuit breaker (named after the electrical switch) stops the calls for a while:

  • Closed (normal): requests flow through. Failures are counted.
  • Open: after too many failures, requests are blocked immediately and the fallback is used. No waiting on a dead service.
  • Half-open: after a cool-down, a few test requests are allowed through. If they succeed, the circuit closes and normal operation resumes. If they fail, it opens again.

Placement prep · Questions you are likely to be asked

Interview questions with answers

Short, precise answers. Read the question, answer it out loud, then open it to check.

Q1What is RAG and why is it needed?Basics

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is a technique where, before an LLM answers, the system retrieves relevant passages from an external knowledge source and adds them to the prompt. It is needed because LLMs only know their training data: they have no access to private or recent information and will hallucinate when asked about it. RAG grounds the answer in real documents.

Q2Explain the R, the A and the G with an example.Basics

Question: “What's the home office reimbursement policy?”

  • Retrieve: search the policy documents and find the home office section.
  • Augment: build a prompt containing that section plus the question.
  • Generate: the LLM writes the answer from that context.
Q3What is hallucination, and how does RAG reduce it?Basics

Hallucination is when an LLM produces a fluent, confident answer that is false or made up. RAG reduces it by giving the model the actual facts and instructing it to answer only from that context, and to say “I don't know” if the context doesn't contain the answer. It reduces hallucination; it does not eliminate it completely.

Q4RAG vs fine-tuning vs prompt engineering: when do you use each?Choosing
  • Prompt engineering: behaviour rules, restrictions, format. Cheap and instant to change.
  • Fine-tuning: stable patterns like style, tone or a domain's writing format. Changes the model's weights.
  • RAG: private, frequently changing facts that need citations.

They are complementary; a production chatbot often uses all three.

Q5Why not fine-tune a model on the company's documents instead of using RAG?Choosing
  • Every document change requires retraining.
  • Training is slow and expensive.
  • No citations, so answers can't be verified.
  • Facts learned in training are recalled unreliably, especially with lots of data.
  • The model has a knowledge cutoff.

RAG retrieves at query time, so an updated document is used on the next question.

Q6What is keyword search and what is its main limitation?Keyword

Keyword (lexical) search ranks documents by how often the query's exact words appear in them. Its limitation is vocabulary mismatch: if the user uses a synonym (“allowance” vs “reimbursement”), the document is not found. It also ignores word meaning and context.

Q7Explain TF-IDF.Keyword

TF-IDF weights each word by term frequency (how often it appears in a document) times inverse document frequency (how rare it is across all documents, roughly log(N/df)). Common words like “office” get low weight; distinctive words like “furniture” get high weight. Documents and queries become TF-IDF vectors compared with cosine similarity.

Q8How is BM25 better than TF-IDF?Keyword
  • Term-frequency saturation (k1): repeated mentions add less and less, so keyword stuffing doesn't win.
  • Document-length normalisation (b): long documents aren't favoured just for having more words.

It is the default ranking in Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and Lucene.

Q9When is keyword search still better than semantic search?Keyword

For exact identifiers and rare terms: error codes, product IDs, names, acronyms. Embeddings can blur these. That is why many systems use hybrid search (BM25 + embeddings).

Q10What is an embedding?Embeddings

An embedding is a fixed-length vector of numbers that represents the meaning of a piece of text. Texts with similar meaning have vectors that are close together, measured with cosine similarity or distance. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 produces 384 numbers per text; OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small produces 1536.

Q11What is semantic search and how does it work?Embeddings

Search by meaning instead of exact words. Steps: embed all documents (or chunks) once; embed the query with the same model; compute similarity between the query vector and document vectors; return the top-k closest.

Q12Dot product, cosine similarity, Euclidean distance: what's the difference?Embeddings
  • Dot product: Σ aᵢbᵢ. Depends on both direction and length.
  • Cosine similarity: dot ÷ (|a||b|). Direction only; range −1 to 1.
  • Euclidean distance: straight-line distance; smaller is more similar.

For unit-length (normalised) vectors, dot product equals cosine, and ranking by Euclidean distance gives the same order.

Q13What does “384-dimensional” and “22 million parameters” mean for all-MiniLM-L6-v2?Embeddings

384-dimensional: each text becomes a list of 384 numbers. 22 million parameters: the model has 22M learned weights. It is ~90 MB, runs on a laptop CPU, and is good enough for sentence similarity, while GPT-class models have billions of parameters because they must generate text and reason.

Q14Local vs API embedding models: trade-offs?Embeddings

Local (sentence-transformers): free, private, offline, but uses your hardware and is often less accurate than the best large models. API (OpenAI, Gemini, Cohere): high quality and no hardware to manage, but costs per token, needs internet, and sends data to a third party.

Q15Can you embed documents with one model and queries with another?Embeddings

No. Each model creates its own vector space (often with different dimensions too). Similarity across two spaces is meaningless. If you change the embedding model you must re-embed every document.

Q16Why use a vector database instead of a NumPy array?Vector DB

Brute-force comparison costs N × d multiplications per query (500 × 384 = 192,000, and millions at real scale). A vector DB provides ANN indexes (HNSW, IVF) for sub-linear search, plus persistence, metadata storage and filtering, updates and deletes, and scaling.

Q17Explain HNSW.Vector DB

Hierarchical Navigable Small World builds a multi-layer graph. Each vector is linked to its nearest neighbours. Upper layers are sparse (long jumps), the bottom layer has every vector. Search starts at an entry point on the top layer, greedily moves to closer neighbours, drops down a layer, and repeats. It is fast and highly accurate, which is why most vector DBs use it by default. The cost is extra memory for the graph.

Q18What is approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) search?Vector DB

Search that returns very likely the closest vectors without checking every one. It trades a small drop in recall for a large speed-up. HNSW, IVF and LSH are ANN methods. Exact (flat) search checks every vector.

Q19Chroma vs Pinecone vs Weaviate?Vector DB

Chroma: open source, Python-first, runs locally, great for learning and prototypes. Pinecone: fully managed cloud service, pay per use, production scale. Weaviate: open source with a GraphQL API, self-host or cloud. Start with Chroma; move to a managed or production-grade DB for scale.

Q20What's the difference between chromadb.Client() and PersistentClient()?Vector DB

Client() is in-memory: all data is lost when the program stops. PersistentClient(path="...") saves to disk so data survives restarts.

Q21What is chunking and why is it needed?Chunking

Splitting documents into smaller pieces before embedding. One embedding for a 50-page document blurs all its topics together, and retrieval would return the whole thing. Chunks give precise retrieval, fit in the LLM's context window, and reduce prompt cost.

Q22How do you choose chunk size and overlap?Chunking

Too small loses context; too large loses precision. Start around 200–500 characters with 50–100 characters overlap (10–20%), split at sentence or paragraph boundaries, then tune by testing real questions and checking whether the retrieved chunks contain the answer. Different content (code, legal text, FAQs) needs different sizes.

Q23Why add overlap between chunks?Chunking

So a sentence or idea that falls on a chunk boundary still appears whole in at least one chunk. Without overlap, “Dogs are allowed” and “on Fridays” could be separated and neither chunk answers the question.

Q24Name some chunking strategies.Chunking

Fixed-size (characters or tokens), sentence-based, paragraph-based, recursive character splitting (LangChain's default: paragraphs → lines → sentences → words), semantic chunking (split where meaning changes), and agentic chunking (an LLM decides the splits).

Q25Walk me through a complete RAG pipeline.Pipeline

Offline: load documents → chunk → embed each chunk → store vector + text + metadata in a vector DB. Online: embed the question with the same model → retrieve top-k chunks → build a prompt (instructions + chunks + question) → LLM generates the answer, ideally with citations.

Q26What goes into the augmented prompt?Pipeline

A role and rules (answer only from context, say “I don't know” otherwise, cite sources, topics to refuse), the retrieved chunks labelled with their sources, and the user's question.

Q27How do you update the knowledge base when a policy changes?Pipeline

Re-chunk and re-embed only the changed document, then upsert those chunks by id and delete the old ones. No model retraining is needed. Also clear or expire cached answers that depended on it.

Q28What is top-k and how do you choose it?Pipeline

k is how many chunks are retrieved per question. Small k may miss the answer; large k adds noise, cost and can confuse the LLM. Typical values are 3–5, tuned by testing. Reranking lets you retrieve many and keep only the best few.

Q29How would you make a RAG system faster?Production

Caching at four levels (query, embedding, vector search, LLM response) in Redis with a TTL; ANN indexes; smaller embedding models; fewer, smaller chunks in the prompt; streaming the LLM output.

Q30Exact cache vs semantic cache?Production

An exact cache keys on a hash of the (normalised) question, so only identical questions hit. A semantic cache embeds the question and reuses an answer if a cached question is similar above a threshold, e.g. cosine ≥ 0.9. Semantic caches hit more often but risk returning an answer to a subtly different question.

Q31What would you monitor in a RAG system?Production

General: latency, throughput, error rate. RAG-specific: retrieval quality (relevance of returned chunks), embedding time, chunking efficiency, cache hit rate, LLM token cost. Alert on thresholds set from real data (e.g. latency > 2 s, errors > 5%).

Q32What is graceful degradation? What is a circuit breaker?Production

Graceful degradation: when a component fails, fall back to a simpler working path (keyword search, raw chunks, text match, friendly error) instead of crashing. A circuit breaker stops calling a failing service after repeated failures (open), then sends a few test requests after a cool-down (half-open) and resumes normal calls (closed) if they succeed.

Q33Describe a production RAG architecture.Production

On Kubernetes: an application layer (web UI, mobile backend, admin), a RAG pipeline layer split into independently scalable microservices (query processing, chunking, embedding, retrieval, augmentation, generation), a data layer (vector DB like Chroma, Redis cache, PostgreSQL metadata) and a monitoring stack (Prometheus metrics, Grafana dashboards, Jaeger tracing, ELK logging).

Q34How do you evaluate whether retrieval is working? Beyond the video

Build a test set of questions with the correct source chunk. Measure recall@k (is the right chunk in the top k?), precision@k (how many of the top k are relevant?) and MRR (how high the first correct chunk ranks). For answers, check faithfulness (is every claim supported by the context?) and answer relevance.
